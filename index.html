Understanding and recognising fake news

You are not gullible — you are busy. Information disorder works because it is engineered to slip past a scrolling brain. This toolkit gives you the vocabulary, the warning signs and a five-minute verification routine you can actually use.

Why this matters

“Fake news” is the wrong phrase for a real problem

The term is too blunt to be useful and too politically loaded to be neutral — it gets used to describe satire, honest mistakes, coordinated state operations, and any coverage someone happens to dislike. Researchers therefore prefer information disorder, which separates what was said from why it was said. That distinction is the whole game: the same false claim needs a different response depending on whether it came from a confused friend or a paid influence operation.

The scale

A European-level response

The European Digital Media Observatory describes itself as the EU's largest interdisciplinary network countering disinformation, coordinating 15 national and multinational hubs across 28 countries in the EU and EEA. Fact-checking is no longer a hobby project — it is infrastructure.

The shift

Cheap to make, expensive to check

Generative AI collapsed the cost of producing a convincing image, voice or article to roughly zero, while verifying one still costs a human minutes or hours. That asymmetry — not any single fake — is what changed. Assume volume, and build a routine instead of relying on instinct.

The catch

Confidence is the risk factor

Most people rate themselves better than average at spotting manipulation. Feeling certain is not evidence — and the strongest predictor of sharing something false is emotional arousal, not lack of education. A checklist beats a gut feeling precisely because it does not care how you feel.

Vocabulary

Three words that do the work of one bad one

This distinction comes from the information-disorder framework developed by Claire Wardle and Hossein Derakhshan. The two variables are simple: is it false? and was harm intended?

01 Misinformation

False, but not intended to harm. Someone shares a wrong number, an out-of-date photo or a misread study because they believe it. Most of what reaches you through people you like is this.

false content · no intent to harm

02 Disinformation

False and deliberately deployed. Fabricated or manipulated content created to deceive, discredit, destabilise or make money. Usually organised, often coordinated across many accounts and languages.

false content · intent to harm

03 Malinformation

True, but weaponised. Genuine information released or reframed to cause damage — leaks timed for an election, private data exposed, a real quote stripped of the context that explained it.

true content · intent to harm

Taxonomy

The seven types of information disorder

Ordered roughly from least to most intentionally deceptive. Naming the technique is often faster than debunking the claim — and it is what fact-checkers do first.

Low intent to deceive

1 · Satire & parody

No intention to harm, but real potential to fool once the joke is separated from its source.

Tell-tale signThe publisher describes itself as satirical somewhere you have to click to find.
Misleading framing

2 · False connection

Headlines, visuals or captions that do not support the content underneath them.

Tell-tale signThe article never actually says what the headline promised.
Misleading framing

3 · Misleading content

Real facts cropped, cherry-picked or reframed to steer you to a conclusion the evidence does not support.

Tell-tale signA chart with a truncated axis, or a percentage with no base number.
Displaced context

4 · False context

Genuine content recirculated with false surrounding information — the most common form you will meet.

Tell-tale signA dramatic photo from a different country or a different year entirely.
Deliberate deception

5 · Imposter content

Genuine sources impersonated — a cloned news logo, a spoofed handle, a fake institutional letterhead.

Tell-tale signThe domain is nearly right: an extra hyphen, .co instead of .com.
Deliberate deception

6 · Manipulated content

Real material altered to deceive — edited images, doctored documents, selectively cut audio and video.

Tell-tale signOnly a screenshot circulates; nobody can produce the original file or post.
Deliberate deception

7 · Fabricated content

100% false, designed to deceive and cause harm — invented studies, whole fake outlets, synthetic media.

Tell-tale signNo trace of the claim anywhere else, in any language, before this post.
Recognition

Twelve red flags — and what to do about each

Select a card to see the countermeasure. None of these proves something is false on its own; two or three together mean stop scrolling and check.

Nothing here is a lie detector. Real reporting sometimes breaks these rules, and a polished fake can follow all of them. Treat red flags as a reason to verify, never as a verdict.

Method

SIFT: four moves, about ninety seconds

Developed by digital-literacy researcher Mike Caulfield and now taught in university libraries across Europe and North America. The point of SIFT is speed — it is designed to be finished before you would have finished reading the article.

Stop

Before anything else, notice your own reaction. If a post made you feel indignant, vindicated or urgently obliged to share, that feeling is the product — it is what the content was optimised to produce.

Two separate stops matter here. The first is emotional: name the feeling, then continue. The second is procedural: if you find yourself an hour deep in a rabbit hole, stop and return to the original question. Most claims do not need a full investigation — they need thirty focused seconds.

Do this

  • Ask: do I actually know this source, or does it merely look familiar?
  • Decide how much verification the claim deserves before you start — a shareable political claim deserves more than a recipe.
  • Do not share while you are still checking. Sharing is the irreversible step.

Investigate the source

You are not judging whether the source looks professional — anyone can look professional. You are asking what other people, outside that source, say about it. This is called lateral reading: leave the page to learn about the page.

Open a new tab and search the outlet’s or author’s name. Read what independent sources say about their funding, ownership and track record. A polished website with a stock-photo “editorial team” and no named journalists tells you a great deal in about twenty seconds.

Do this

  • Search "[outlet name]" + funding / ownership / bias in a new tab.
  • Check whether the outlet appears in a recognised fact-checking or press-standards network.
  • Look for a named author with a traceable history, a physical address, and a corrections policy.
  • For scientific claims, check whether the study is peer-reviewed and who paid for it.

Find better coverage

Often you do not care about this particular article — you care whether the claim is true. So skip the article and go looking for the best available reporting on the claim itself.

If a genuinely significant event happened, more than one independent newsroom will have it, usually in more than one language. If the only sources are the original post and a chain of accounts citing each other, you have found a closed loop, not corroboration.

Do this

  • Search the claim in your own words, not the headline's wording.
  • Check whether a fact-checker has already covered it — most viral claims are recycled.
  • Count genuinely independent sources. Ten outlets all citing the same anonymous post is one source.
  • Search in another language, especially for claims about another country.

Trace claims, quotes and media to the original

Most manipulation happens in transit, not at the source. Content gets stripped of context as it is re-shared: a quote loses its qualifier, a photo loses its date, a study loses its sample size.

So go upstream. Follow the link chain back until you reach the primary document — the actual report, the full video, the original photograph — and check whether it says what the post claims it says. Surprisingly often, the primary source contradicts the post that cites it.

Do this

  • Reverse-image-search photographs to find the earliest version and its original caption.
  • Watch the longer cut of any clip that ends suspiciously well.
  • Open the actual study, report or court document — read the abstract and the limitations.
  • If the original has been deleted, look for an archived copy before accepting a screenshot.

Toolkit

Where to check, and what each tool is good for

All links open externally. Nothing here replaces judgement — in particular, automated “AI detectors” are unreliable in both directions and should never be treated as proof.

The flagship project of the EU's East StratCom Task Force, within the European External Action Service. Maintains a searchable database of catalogued pro-Kremlin disinformation cases and recurring narratives.

The European Digital Media Observatory: fact-checking coordination, media-literacy resources, research and policy analysis, plus monthly reports from its fact-checking network. Start with your national hub.

edmo.eu
EU · NGO

EU DisinfoLab

An independent Brussels-based non-profit focused on disinformation affecting the EU and its member states. Useful for understanding how influence operations are structured rather than just what they claim.

disinfo.eu
Fact-checking

European Fact-Checking Standards Network

The network of European fact-checking organisations working to a shared code of standards. The quickest way to find out whether a signatory near you has already checked the claim.

efcsn.com
Fact-checking

Google Fact Check Explorer

Searches published fact-checks from organisations worldwide in one query. Try it before you write a rebuttal — someone has usually done the work already.

toolbox.google.com/factcheck
Images & video

Reverse image search

Google Lens, Bing Visual Search, Yandex and TinEye each index different parts of the web — run more than one. You are looking for the earliest appearance and its original caption, not the prettiest match.

tineye.com
Images & video

InVID / WeVerify plugin

A browser toolkit built for newsroom verification: splits video into keyframes for reverse search, reads metadata, magnifies detail and checks social posts. The single most useful thing to install.

weverify.eu
Archives

Wayback Machine

Retrieves what a page said before it was quietly edited or deleted, and lets you archive a page yourself so your evidence survives. Essential when only a screenshot is circulating.

web.archive.org
Open-source research

Bellingcat's toolkit

A maintained, categorised list of open-source investigation tools — geolocation, satellite imagery, flight and vessel tracking, corporate records. Deeper than you usually need, invaluable when you do.

Glossary

Terms you will meet

Useful when reading fact-checks, platform transparency reports or EU policy documents.

Astroturfing

Manufacturing the appearance of grassroots support. A coordinated campaign — sometimes automated, sometimes paid humans — is dressed up as spontaneous public opinion. Tells include accounts created in the same week, near-identical phrasing, and sudden bursts of activity at office hours in one time zone.

Confirmation bias

The tendency to accept evidence that fits what we already believe and to scrutinise evidence that does not. This is not a defect of unintelligent people; it is how everyone processes information by default. The practical consequence: apply your strictest checks to the claims you most want to be true.

Content Credentials (C2PA)

A technical standard for attaching tamper-evident provenance data to media — what device or software made it, and how it was edited since. Support is growing across cameras and editing tools, but absence of credentials proves nothing on its own, since metadata is routinely stripped when files are uploaded.

Deepfake / synthetic media

Audio, image or video generated or altered by machine learning to depict something that did not happen. Visual artefacts are becoming an unreliable tell as models improve, so verification is shifting towards provenance and corroboration: does any independent record of this event exist? Voice cloning is currently the cheapest and most effective variety, and is widely used in fraud.

Digital Services Act (DSA)

EU regulation setting obligations for online platforms on illegal content, transparency of recommender systems and advertising, and risk assessment. It has applied to platforms across the EU since February 2024, with stricter duties for very large platforms and search engines. The EU's Code of Practice on Disinformation was subsequently converted into a code of conduct within this framework.

Filter bubble / echo chamber

A filter bubble is what algorithmic personalisation shows you; an echo chamber is what your social circle repeats back. They compound each other, and both make a fringe claim feel like consensus. The countermeasure is deliberate, not passive: seek out competent sources you disagree with.

Lateral reading

Evaluating a source by leaving it — opening new tabs to see what independent parties say about it — rather than by studying the page itself. Professional fact-checkers do this reflexively; students, by default, do not. It is the single highest-yield habit on this page.

Prebunking / inoculation

Explaining a manipulation technique before someone encounters it, so the tactic is recognised rather than the specific claim debunked afterwards. Evidence suggests technique-based prebunking generalises better than fact-by-fact correction — which is why the “red flags” section above is framed around methods.

Sea-lioning

Exhausting an opponent through relentless, superficially polite demands for evidence, with no intention of being persuaded. Recognising it matters because the appropriate response — disengaging — looks like losing the argument to onlookers.
